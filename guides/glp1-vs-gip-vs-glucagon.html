GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptors: what each arm does in a metabolic study

Single, dual and triple agonists are different experiments, not different doses. Here is what each receptor arm contributes and which compound isolates it.

Updated 27 Nov 2026 · Reviewed by the Peflag QC desk · Release method

Short answer. GLP-1R drives glucose-dependent insulin release and slows gastric emptying; GIPR adds an insulinotropic and adipose-lipid arm; GCGR raises hepatic glucose output and energy expenditure. A single agonist isolates one arm, a dual two, and a triple three.

GLP-1R, GIPR and GCGR drive different physiology, and a study result is only interpretable if you know which arm you engaged. The table maps each receptor to its main research read-outs and to the compounds in this catalogue that act on it.

Why dual and triple agonists exist

Adding a receptor arm is not a dose increase; it is a different pharmacology. A dual agonist produces a response that a higher dose of a single agonist cannot reproduce, because the second receptor contributes its own signalling and its own side-effect profile.

That is also why the comparator matters. A triple-agonist result measured against vehicle tells you the compound works; measured against a GLP-1-selective comparator, it tells you what the extra arms add.

What actually changes in the read-outs

The glucagon arm changes hepatic glucose output and energy expenditure; the amylin arm changes satiety signalling and gastric emptying. If a study measures only body weight, the arms look interchangeable — which is why the read-out panel has to match the receptor claim.

Three design mistakes that make the data unreadable

Most receptor-comparison studies fail on design, not on compound quality. Three mistakes account for most of the results nobody can interpret.

  • No single-incretin arm. Without semaglutide or liraglutide as a control, the added GIP or glucagon effect cannot be separated from a dose effect.
  • Milligram matching. Matching milligrams instead of moles across compounds of different mass changes exposure between arms.
  • One read-out. Body weight alone makes dual and triple agonists look interchangeable; add glycaemia, intake or energy expenditure.

Running several arms in one study

One assay day, one vehicle and one lot of cells or animals per read-out is worth more than a bigger n. Arms compared across different weeks carry a batch effect that no statistical test removes afterwards.

Buyer questions

GLP-1 vs GIP vs glucagon — questions buyers ask

What is the difference between GLP-1 and GIP?

GLP-1 slows gastric emptying and drives glucose-dependent insulin release; GIP is primarily insulinotropic and also acts on adipose lipid handling. A GLP-1-selective compound isolates the first arm; a dual agonist engages both.

Why add glucagon to an incretin agonist?

The glucagon arm raises hepatic glucose output and energy expenditure, which changes the metabolic read-out in a way that GLP-1 and GIP alone do not. That is why triple agonists are studied separately from dual agonists rather than as a dose increase.

Which compound should be the control arm?

A GLP-1-selective compound — semaglutide or liraglutide — is the standard single-incretin control. Without it, a dual- or triple-agonist result cannot be attributed to the added receptor arm.

Do these differences change the analytical method?

No. Identity and purity release is the same for every compound: RP-HPLC at 220 nm and ESI-MS mass. The pharmacology differs; the release method does not.

Are these compounds interchangeable at matched milligrams?

No. Milligram doses are not molar-equivalent across compounds of different mass, and potency at a receptor is not proportional to mass. Plan around molar concentrations when comparing arms.
