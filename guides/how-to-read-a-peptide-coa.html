How to read a peptide COA: HPLC, mass spec, water and fill weight

A certificate of analysis is only useful if you can check it. Six fields decide whether a peptide batch is releasable — and six red flags say it is not.

Updated 27 Nov 2026 · Reviewed by the Peflag QC desk · Release method

Short answer. A releasable peptide COA contains six checkable fields: a batch number, an HPLC chromatogram with method and area-normalised purity, an ESI-MS spectrum with deconvoluted mass, Karl Fischer water content, fill weight per vial, and a storage statement. Anything less is a specification sheet, not a COA.

The six fields a releasable COA must contain

A COA is checkable or it is decoration. Six fields carry the decision: batch number, HPLC purity with chromatogram, ESI-MS identity, water content, fill weight, and storage conditions.

How HPLC purity is calculated, and what it does not measure

Purity in a peptide COA is the area of the main peak divided by the total peak area, measured at 220 nm on a reversed-phase C18 column. A 99.2% result means 0.8% of the UV signal sits in other peaks — not that 0.8% of the powder is a different peptide.

Three things follow from that definition. First, the method has to be stated: column, gradient, wavelength and run time change the number. Second, water and counter-ions are invisible at 220 nm, so water content and fill weight are separate tests, not optional extras. Third, a chromatogram with a clean baseline and a single symmetrical main peak is more informative than any percentage on its own.

Reading the mass spectrum

Identity is confirmed when the deconvoluted mass matches the theoretical mass within 0.1%. For semaglutide, 4113.58 g/mol is the target; a result at 4113.6 is a pass, a result 40 Da away is a different molecule.

Expect to see several charge states in the raw spectrum (for example [M+4H]⁴⁺ and [M+5H]⁵⁺). The deconvolution collapses them into one mass. Sodium and potassium adducts are normal and appear as small satellites; a large adduct cluster usually means the sample was run in the wrong solvent, not that the peptide is wrong.

Water content and fill weight

Karl Fischer water content above 5.0% means you are paying for moisture, and fill weight tells you how much powder is actually in each vial. Both are reported per batch because lyophilisation removes water unevenly across a tray.

  • Water: ≤5.0% by Karl Fischer at release. Hygroscopic peptides drift upward once a vial is opened.
  • Fill weight: the observed range across the kit, not a nominal figure. A 10 mg vial that contains 9.4–9.9 mg is normal; a range of 7–12 mg is not.
  • Reconciliation: fill weight × (1 − water) gives the peptide mass you can actually weigh out.

What a COA does not tell you

A COA says nothing about sterility, endotoxin, biological activity or the identity of individual impurities. It is an identity-and-purity record for a research chemical, and treating it as a pharmaceutical release document is a category error.

If a study needs impurity profiling, residual solvent data or a bioassay, those are separate methods with separate reports. Ask before ordering rather than assuming the COA covers them.

Six red flags worth walking away from

  • A COA that is identical for two different batches — same chromatogram image, same numbers.
  • Purity quoted to two decimals with no method and no chromatogram.
  • No water content field, or a water figure of "0.0%".
  • Mass "confirmed" without a deconvoluted value.
  • Fill weight given as a single nominal number.
  • The COA arrives only after payment, and only when asked.

Comparing two COAs side by side

Put both documents next to each other and compare four numbers: purity, mass error, water and fill-weight range. One difference is a question to ask; four differences describe two different processes.

  • Purity: a 0.3-point gap can be method rather than quality — check the wavelength and gradient before judging.
  • Mass: compare the error in daltons, not the rounded value. 0.4 Da at 4,800 Da is a pass; 4 Da is not.
  • Water: 3% versus 5% water changes the peptide mass you can weigh out by roughly 2%.
  • Fill weight: compare the width of the range; a tight range implies per-vial verification.

Five questions to ask before the first order

  1. Can I see the COA for the exact batch you will ship, before payment?
  2. Which HPLC method and wavelength produced the purity figure?
  3. Is water measured by Karl Fischer, and what is the acceptance limit?
  4. Is fill weight verified per vial or per kit?
  5. Who signs the release, and can I see the method record?

A supplier who answers all five in one reply is describing a process, not a product.

Buyer questions

How to read a peptide COA — questions buyers ask

Is 99% HPLC purity the same as 99% peptide content?

No. HPLC purity is the share of UV-absorbing peak area at 220 nm, not the share of peptide mass. Water, counter-ions and residual salts are invisible to the detector, which is why a separate Karl Fischer water test and a fill-weight check are part of the same release record.

Why do you send the COA before payment?

Because the batch is chosen by the buyer, not by the invoice. Sending the chromatogram, the mass spectrum and the fill-weight range first lets a QA group decide whether the batch is acceptable before any money moves.

Can a third party verify the batch?

Yes — testing can be arranged at an ISO/IEC 17025-accredited laboratory, with the report issued in your institution's name. It typically adds about five working days and a testing fee, and is the usual route when an internal SOP requires an independent result.

What happens if a batch fails release?

It is held, not discounted. Batches below 99.0% purity, outside the mass tolerance, above 5.0% water or outside the fill-weight range do not enter the catalogue; there is no commercial override on a release decision.

Does the COA cover sterility or endotoxin?

No. The standard COA covers identity, purity, water content and fill weight. These are research chemicals, not sterile injectables; sterility and endotoxin testing are not part of the release and should not be assumed.
